## A Kobayashi Maru?
A no-win situation - a test! In the Star Trek, The Wrath of Khan, we see Starfleet cadets undergo this test - commanding a simulated ship towards inevitable, crushing defeat in an unwinnable scenario.

Although the outcome will always be crushing defeat, the test aims to assess _how_ cadets handle such a situation. Do they make decisions well? Do they handle the situation with grace? Do they minimise their losses, despite the unfavourable outcome?

Today's scenario falls firmly into this category, and we'll focus on identifying and responding to it through this lens.

# How to Structure Your Thinking
Prevention is the best countermeasure for tough situations at work. 

To that end, let's look at how you can develop your tactical mindset when it comes to politics, without losing your sanity! 

## 1. The Ladder of Inference
At the end of the day, "tactical awareness" in a political sense mostly comes from your ability to tie the actions of people around you to their motives, needs, and larger picture intentions. 
<!-- TODO - rephrase this, use a different term to tactical awareness ^^ -->

The ladder of inference is a tool that can help you to do this. The intention behind it is not to change your thoughts necessarily, just to make you more conscious of how you think, and how you're drawing conclusions from observations you make

### How it works
We step through each run of a ladder. 

1. **Selected Reality** - Specific and objective facts / observations
2. **Interpretations** - Your immediate interpretation of those facts
3. **Assumptions** - Assumptions you've drawn on, in addition to the facts
4. **Conclusions** - The conclusion your interpretation and assumptions have led you to
5. **Beliefs** - How that conclusion has affected your thinking / feelings
6. **Actions** - The actions you take as a result

Different online resources have slightly inconsistent steps and ways of describing those steps to one another, some being a little too vague or buzzwordy to be of practical use. Like most things theory, it tends to make more sense with something more tangible.

## 2. Tactical Vision

In chess, "vision" is your ability to recognise the state of the pieces on the board, and assess what is likely to happen a number of moves into the future after you make your current move.

It's a vital skill to learn up on when playing chess. For example, looking at this flipped board from a recent game I played:

<!-- CHESS FEN IMG -->

There's a checkmate in 2 for white, but it's not necessarily an obvious one (if the board wasn't as clear as this one, since I've removed all the irrelevant pieces!). We should recognise that white is playing for checkmate, and then see:
1. White sacrifices their queen with a check: `Qe6+`. 
2. Since black's king is in check, their only legal move is the take the queen with their bishop: `Bxe6`
3. White now moves their rook in front of the black king. Since it's protected by the white bishop, this is checkmate: `Re7#`

## Why Are We Playing Chess?
Don't worry - this isn't a chess post, and you don't need to know anything about chess to read on!

The takeaway from this short snippet is to recognise a few things:
- **White's intentions are to _checkmate_**
    - This isn't always the case. Sometimes a player will play for a stalemate, or may not have seen far enough ahead to be playing for anything - instead just opportunistically taking and defending their pieces

- **Black's intentions _aren't clear_**
    - Even though they do make a move, is a forced one, so we can't really infer anything from this game about what they want to do

- **Without the vision to see the checkmate in 2, white's first move could appear to just be a queen blunder**
    - The example above is a pretty basic one in which black can't actually do anything to stop mate
        - If however black did have some chance to defend, but does not see the threat of checkmate from `Re7#` to apply that defence, they will get checkmated all the same
    - Conversely, if white did genuinely blunder their queen and did not see the checkmate in 1, they may play some different move to `Re7#` and allow black to escape

## Cool! So... How Does This Help Us?
You may already be connecting the dots on this one. 

Chess involves a lot of tactics and forward thinking. Knowing what the other player is intending to do is essential, as is knowing how the moves that they make align with those intentions.

In any situation where political play is involved (realistically, most situations), there are a some questions you can ask to help develop your tactical vision. Like most things we'll talk about here, these are far from comprehensive.

### Knowing someone's intentions
- What does this person want in the short term? 
    - _white wants to attack the black king_

- What drives this person in the longer term? 
    - _white is driven by the opportunity to deliver checkmate_

- What are they prepared to sacrifice to get what they want? 
    - _white performs a literal queen sacrifice_

- What will they not allow to be taken from them? 
    - _white does not want to sacrifice / lose their rook. They need it for eventual mate_

- How does this person typically operate?
    - _when white is playing for checkmate, they like to do so up-close to the king rather than from far away_

# Once Things Have Gone Awry

## How a Personal Animus Can Be Expressed in a Work Environment
Personal problems happen - it's not a function of who you are or how you operate, at some point in you career it's quite likely someone will form some personal animus towards you. Sometimes you'll be able to determine the source of that animus, and you use that to work out a peaceful solution, but this of course may not always be the case.

What can be most dangerous is someone with an opaque reason to act with hostility towards you, who knows how to express those feelings politically!

### A Document of "Feedback"
In our scenario, we'll talk about "Damian", who expresses this as a fairly hefty written document of "feedback" that is first escalated to our manager, and then later shared with us. There importantly aren't any early warning signs of this document, and we don't hear from Damian about his thoughts and feelings before this goes up to our manager.

We'll observe that the document is split into a few sections, each named after a company value, and contains some pretty pointed language. There are quotes from it like "I'll be blunt with this one...", "You wasted valuable time trying to...", and "I'll end this with two questions. \<_2x rhetorical questions follow_>". 

With titles like "Respect", "Trust", and "Complexity" (a topical phrase at that org), we see that the document seems to be aiming to be as eye-catching to senior leadership as possible. There's a lot of content within it, but in a broad sense it:
- Tends to center around the theme of feeling disrespected / not trusted
- Links to discussions that Damian felt were disrespectful or dismissive of his thoughts

Even without substantial vision we can tell that Damian is quite unhappy. We can also make use of the assumptions that because we've had a good working relationship with Damian for about a year, he must be giving sincere feedback.

This is where our thinking is at the time, and it leads us to the action that we try to investigate and apply the feedback as though it were sincere feedback. Since much of the feedback is novel, and seems to actually be mentioning things we otherwise get praised for as our strengths, we should investigate further. This is always a good idea - often receiving feedback like this (where sincere) could be a sign that others around you have made this observation but not shared it as feedback with you. Collecting various perspectives to understand and implement improvements is how you become better at your job, no matter what that is. 

We'll find however, that is was possibly the worst thing we could've done in this scenario! We reach out to Damian a little later, to check in on how we're doing with applying their feedback, and to ask for clarity on much of the points raised since we weren't able to find anyone else who seemed to agree with them. 

This deeply offends Damian, who feels that rather than understand his feedback better, we've actually tried to undermine the feedback!

### Applying our Vision
<!-- TODO: shorten this section, make it more punchy -->
That was about where my understanding ended and my descent into the Kobayashi Maru began at the time. But with the benefit of hindsight and newly trained vision, we can form a deeper understanding of what Damian's "feedback" document represented, and what he wanted to happen.

The key thing to really drill into is where the document comes from - it has nothing to do with feedback, that was just the most socially acceptable way for Damian to share + draw wider attention to their real thought, being:

_"I feel disrespected by you, and I don't like it"_

It contains a lot of emotional language, and a common theme among example discussions Damian links is the fact that they seem completely benign to anyone else that you share it with to ask for clarity. This is critical! 

What Damian wanted you to take away from this is their feelings of being disrespected. They likely wanted to be heard, and likely wanted you to apologise to them (as we'll see later, this is expected in a very specific form as well <!-- TODO: make sure this link is made later --> ). The action was not for you to share their feedback around in an attempt to understand it, it was for you to understand their feelings and respond to those.

One key indicator of this is the way that the document tends to use the linked examples. We'll observe that they're used like justifications, rather than explanations. It reads more like a legal document than a friendly feedback document.

A good way to confirm this, so you don't misinterpret real feedback, is to think about how it would sound if this were something you gave to one of your juniors / anyone you work with. It's quite hard to imagine giving a document like to someone expecting it could help them in any way!

And on top of this of course is how the document seems intentionally geared towards getting the attention of your leaders, and their leaders above. 
- If themed sections were necessary, why align a document like this to org cultural values? 
- Why share it with your manager and their senior manager before sharing anything with you?
- Why are very old conversations being included as examples, and why wasn't anything raised during the time of those conversations?

Begin with the assumption that a document like this is sincere, and both question a document like this with things that feel inconsistent + do your level best to see if you can find answers that still align with that idea of sincerity. If you can, that's great, and if you can't, you should recognise that special attention is needed.

Now of course, this can be very hard to see in the moment, especially in a case like this where it comes quite suddenly after what feels like an otherwise positive working relationship. The sudden-ness is fairly expected, and usually a result of built up frustrations and animosity finally having an outlet (in this case, they can be expressed under the guise of professional feedback).
